
# Hormozi — Offer & Price

## 1. Role in the Closer Brain

This skill owns the middle-to-late section of the call: from "can I tell you how we'd solve this?" through the price, the guarantee, payment options, the money objection, and collecting payment. In Hormozi's CLOSER terms it owns **S** (Sell the vacation) and the money side of **E** (Explain away concerns), and it borrows from **C/L/O** only the facts it needs to price against.

Hand off:
- **Miner** owns discovery (Clarify, Label, Overview past pain). Take his output; flag if discovery is too thin to price against (no quantified gap, no cost of inaction, no timeline).
- **Belfort** owns tone, certainty and control of the call. Hand off when the delivery, not the words, is the problem.
- **Daniel G** owns the close mechanics and repeated asks once the price is out.
- **Voss** owns non-money objections and tactical empathy (spouse, "think about it", trust). Keep money/value/price objections here; route the rest.
- **Owen Santos** owns full-call reviews. This skill scores only the offer/price stage and feeds him.

## 2. Core principles (and why they work)

1. **Price is only "too high" relative to perceived value.** "Nothing is ever too expensive, it's just not worth it" (Hormozi). His Ferrari test: if you offered your Ferrari for $5,000 cash within 24 hours, a broke person would find the money. So "I can't afford it" means value wasn't communicated, and the rep owns the fix, not the prospect.
2. **Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice).** On a call you can't change the product, but you control how much of each variable the prospect *perceives*. Make the top side bigger and specific, and make the bottom side feel small. Hormozi says the bottom side (speed, ease) is where fortunes are made, because promising bigger is easy and everyone does it.
3. **You're selling against doing nothing, not against competitors.** The biggest rival is the comfort of staying the same. Lower their "action threshold" until stepping over it is easier than staying put. That's why guarantees and the cost of inaction carry so much weight.
4. **Customers default to price when you give them nothing else to compare.** "It's always price if they don't know what else to talk about." Make it apples vs. oranges: bring up variables they hadn't thought of (risk, speed, quality, support) so they see they'd be getting less for less.
5. **Obstacles before price, objections after.** An obstacle is a concern you handle before the number. Once it shows up after the number it's an objection, and those are far harder because "now they're like, he's trying to sell me." Kill the zombies up front.
6. **Sell the vacation, not the plane flight.** People buy Maui (the result), not the TSA line (modules, calls, logins, process). Keep the pitch to about three minutes, built on three pillars and short stories. Features sound like work.
7. **Tie the price to their goal.** When the prospect picks where they are and where they want to be, "the price came from them." (He learned this from self-serve yogurt: same price, but you filled the cup, so you own it.)
8. **Anchor high and go for the gasp.** "If you didn't get a gasp from the price tag, you didn't go high enough." Saying the biggest number first makes every later number feel small. Show the full price first and let the discount make you the good guy.
9. **Never change price to close; change terms.** "We don't negotiate with terrorists." A discount with no reason tells them every price is negotiable, and they'll tell everyone. Payment plans, financing and feature downsells are fine. Price cuts are not.
10. **Logic makes the sale stick.** Emotion gets them to act, but logic stops the buyer's remorse two days later. Give their logical brain the math so it can justify a decision they already want to make.
11. **Conviction carries the price.** "Selling is a transference of belief over a bridge of trust." A rep who wouldn't sell it to their mother will flinch at the number. Reread testimonials before you dial.

## 3. The framework, in call order

**Step 0. Collect price ammo (from Miner's discovery).** Before you can price, you need: current state, desired state (in their words, with numbers), what's in the way, what they've tried and why it failed, what the problem costs them now ("if you had to put a number on it..."), what five more years of this looks like, the time they can commit, who else decides, and how they usually pay for things. If any of these is missing, go back for it. Don't present.

**Step 1. Kill zombies (obstacles before price).** Before the offer, bring up the usual blockers as neutral questions. "How much time each week could you realistically put toward this?" "Is anyone else part of a decision like this?" "If this is the right fit, are you in a position to invest in it today?" Lock in their answers. After the price, "what changed between then and now?" (head tilt, curious tone) brings them back to what they already told you.

**Step 2. Bridge.** "Based on what you told me, can I show you how I think we can get you there?" Wait for the yes. That yes is permission to present.

**Step 3. Sell the vacation (3 minutes max).** Restate their gap in their words, then explain it with three pillars ("there are three things that have to be true for you to get X: A, B and C"). Use a short story or analogy for each pillar, not features. Show how each pillar fixes something they tried before ("that's why it didn't work last time, you had A but not C"). Use proof the prospect can relate to: "someone just like you…". Keep it about them, not the company.

**Step 4. Translate the offer into the value equation.** Hit all four levers in one or two sentences each:
- *Dream outcome*: their goal, in their numbers.
- *Likelihood*: proof, track record, and the guarantee.
- *Time*: the first quick win, and when the full result arrives.
- *Effort*: what you do for them, what they don't have to do, and what gets taken off their plate.

**Step 5. Present the price in relation to the goal.** Brace first, then anchor, then tie to the outcome, then stop talking.
- Brace: "Okay, it is expensive." (Hormozi picked this up from Vegas helicopter sellers. A wealthy prospect pictures a number bigger than yours, and a stretched one is braced for it.)
- Anchor: say the full investment (the payment-plan or full-term figure) first.
- Tie it to the outcome: "So when you invest X, you're putting [their goal] behind you, and we'll get there one way or another as long as you follow the steps."
- Silence. Let them react first. "Wow, that's a lot" is a reaction, not an objection. Don't start handling it.

**Step 6. Reverse the risk.** Spell out the guarantee in plain words: what they do, what you promise, and what happens if you miss. Don't just say "results guaranteed." If there's no guarantee, use the anti-guarantee: "If you need a guarantee, this isn't for you. I can't do the work for you." Frame the guarantee as how they get to make an *informed* decision: "You can't make an informed decision until you're on the inside."

**Step 7. Step down the payment options (one at a time, highest first).**
1. Prepay with the biggest discount. Call it a "prepayment discount", never "pay in full" or "PIF" (Hormozi: that's a salesman's term, not a customer benefit).
2. Third-party financing if you have it.
3. Partial (e.g. half down) with a smaller discount.
4. Payment plan at the full-boat price.
5. A smaller commitment or the next step, so "everyone buys something".
Present the full price first and the discounts after, so you're the good guy for saving them money, not the bad guy charging extra because they can't pay.

**Step 8. Get the yes, then stop talking.** Once they say yes, ask for payment details and go quiet. Every extra detail is a chance to turn the yes into a maybe. Use assumptive language ("Do you want to use the card ending 4421?") and in person ask for ID first, then payment.

**Step 9. Handle the money objection by working the value.** Isolate it, then use one to three of the money frames (Section 4 / playbook). Resolve it, then ask again. Repeat as many times as needed, as long as you actually resolved something each time.

**Step 10. Reinforce (the R).** Confirm the decision, show them what happens next and who they'll meet. Remorse sets in within 48 hours if they're ignored.

## 4. Word tracks

*Bridge and pitch*
- "Based on everything you said, can I show you how I think we'd get you from [current] to [desired]?"
- "There are three things that have to be true for you to [goal]: A, B, and C. You've had A. You've never had C. That's why it hasn't stuck."
- "Everyone gets to Maui. The only question is whether you swim, take the boat, or take the jet." (for tiers)

*Price presentation*
- "Okay, it is expensive." (Hormozi; say it before the number, then pause)
- "You said you want [goal] by [date]. The full program to get you there is [full price]. That means [goal] is handled. You can put it out of your mind."
- "If I can save you a little money, do you want me to?" (leads into the prepayment discount)
- "Most people take the prepayment discount. Do you want to use the card you already gave me?"

*Guarantee*
- "Here's exactly what I'll put in writing: if you do [2–3 controllable actions] and you don't [specific outcome] by [date], [what we'll do]."
- "Best case, you [dream outcome]. Worst case, [guarantee], and you got [period] of help for free. Both options are risk-free. Walking away is the only one guaranteed not to get you there. Which risk-free option do you want?" (Hormozi's best-case/worst-case; he says he has closed more sales with it than with any other close)
- No guarantee: "If you're the kind of person who needs a guarantee, this honestly isn't for you. I can't do the work for you. Does that sound fair?"

*"It's too expensive / I can't afford it"*
- "Is this a lot of money for you?" ... "Good. The people for whom it's a lot are the ones who try hardest, and they're our best stories."
- "If this did exactly what we said, got you [goal], would it be worth it?" ... "Then it's not about the price. It's whether you believe it'll work for you. Let's talk about that."
- "You're going to pay for this either way: with money, or with years of figuring it out yourself. Which do you want to spend?"
- "If I handed you the keys to a Ferrari for five grand, would you find the five grand?" ... "So it's not about the money. It's that you don't see this as worth it yet. Which part don't you believe?"
- "Resourceful, not resources. Have you ever had a surprise bill you somehow covered? You found it because it mattered."
- "What else would you spend this money on in the next 12 months that would get you closer to [goal]?"

*Negotiation and competitor*
- "Can you do it for less?" → "We could do it for more." (Hormozi says this counter-anchor has "never not worked" for him.) Then: "I don't change prices, but I can change how you pay."
- "If ours cost the same as theirs, which would you pick?" ... "Why?" ... "Right, and that's why we cost more." (the cheap-comparison close)
- "They're charging less because they're doing less. You wouldn't be getting the same thing for less. You'd be getting less for less."

*Stalls touching money*
- "What are you most afraid of happening if you do this?"
- "Let's play it out. Worst case, [X]. You've been there before and you were fine. Best case, [Y] for good. Is that a bet you're willing to take?"
- "Don't let a bad decision burn you twice: once when that program didn't work, and again when it stops you from the one that will."

## 5. Tone and delivery

- **Brace, say it, then go silent.** Slow down for the number and say it flat and certain, not apologetically. Never rush past it or tack a discount onto the same breath. Whoever talks first after the price usually gives something up.
- **Calm, low voice for the important parts.** Hormozi deliberately drops his tone "so that you listen to the words."
- **Curious, not combative.** "Huh, that's interesting. Tell me more." Tilt your head (even on the phone it changes your voice). It also buys you two seconds. Never argue: "If you win the argument, you lose the sale."
- **Humor to defuse, then switch tones.** A light, teasing line (on his "you said four hours a week, what happened to my four hours?") lowers tension. Then shift to serious: "What's really going on?"
- **Sell from the back foot.** Act like you're fine either way ("I'm good, I want to help you help you"). Commission breath (sounding like you need the deal) kills trust.
- **Want the gasp.** Train yourself to expect it, even enjoy it. If the rep flinches at the number, the prospect will too.
- **Ratio.** The prospect should do about two-thirds of the talking. Your talking goes into the three-minute pitch and the price.

## 6. Mistakes Hormozi calls out (and the fix)

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Pitching before the problem is clear ("8 minutes in and I don't know their revenue") | No price until the gap and the cost of inaction are stated in their words and numbers |
| Talking product (modules, calls, platform), "the plane flight" | Three pillars plus stories, under three minutes, all aimed at their goal |
| Leaving obstacles until after the price | Ask about time, the decision maker and access to money before the price, and lock in the answers |
| Quoting the low or discounted price first and then adding interest for payment plans | Anchor on the full price, then hand out discounts for prepaying |
| Saying "pay in full" or "PIF" | "Prepayment discount": put the benefit in the customer's words |
| Dropping the price to close | Change terms (plan, financing, downsell), never the price. Use "we could do it for more" |
| Vague "results guaranteed" | Spell out the conditions, outcome, date and remedy, in plain words |
| Treating "wow, that's expensive" as an objection and over-handling | Treat it as a reaction: "It is. And…" Keep going unless they actually say no |
| Talking after the yes | Ask for payment, then stop talking |
| Asking "do you have any questions?" | Ask questions you already know the answers to. Don't hand them the wheel |
| Prejudging who can afford it | Sell everyone the same. "Their money spends the same" |
| Answering a trap question directly | Ask a question back about their question, then answer (3A: acknowledge, associate, ask) |
| Asking again without resolving anything | Resolve the concern, then ask. You can do this unlimited times without it getting pushy |
| Low conviction (rep doesn't believe the price is fair) | Reread testimonials daily, and know the outcome is worth more than the price |

## 7. How to use this skill

**Prep mode (lead notes or setter notes in):** Output (a) the price ammo you have and what's missing, to get in discovery; (b) the 3-pillar pitch for this prospect, with one story each; (c) value-equation lines for their case; (d) the exact price sentence tied to their goal; (e) the guarantee wording; (f) the payment step-down order; (g) the two or three most likely money objections for this profile, each with the frame you'd lead with. Keep it to one screen.

**Live / next-line mode (transcript snippet in):** Work out which step they're on (Section 3) and whether the last line was a *reaction*, an *obstacle* or an *objection*. Give the exact next line in quotes, one sentence on why (which lever or frame it pulls), and a fallback if they push back. Keep it short enough to read mid-call. If the problem is discovery, tone or a non-money stall, say which expert to switch to.

**Call-review mode (full transcript in):** Find the offer/price section (from the bridge through payment or the end). Score it against the scorecard below and quote the moments. If the price was never presented, score Price Presentation 1 and explain what stopped it. Be specific and blunt. No generic praise.

## A. The value equation, used on a call

**Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)**

Write it like a fraction: two variables on top that create value, two on the bottom that destroy it. Raise the top and shrink the bottom.

| Lever | What it is | How a closer raises perceived value live |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | The result they actually want (status-linked outcomes are worth more: "make money" beats "lose weight" for most men) | Use their words and numbers. Make the outcome personal: what it means for them, not what it is |
| Perceived likelihood | How sure they are that *they* will get it | Proof from someone like them, volume (the doctor with 10,000 surgeries charges more than the one with 1, even when he's faster), the guarantee, and "why it didn't work before" (you were missing pillar C) |
| Time delay | Time from paying to getting a result, plus how soon they get the first win | Name the first quick win and when it lands. Hormozi: "deliver the same thing in half the time" is a business in itself. Netflix vs Blockbuster, Amazon next-day |
| Effort & sacrifice | What they must start doing that they don't want to, and stop doing that they like | Spell out what you do for them. Two agencies: one needs 4 hrs/week of your input and 60 days, the other has your phone ringing the day you sign. Same product, very different value |

**Why it matters for price:** a $50k liposuction and a $5 ebook make the same promise (weight loss). The price gap is entirely likelihood, time and effort. When a prospect says "I can get X cheaper", they're comparing dream outcomes and ignoring the bottom half. Your job is to show them the bottom half.

**Value equation as a claim (copy formula):** dream outcome + a number of people who achieved it + how long it took + the stipulation that drops effort. For example: "70% of [people like you] get [result] in their first 30 days without having to [hated task]." Use the same structure verbally when you sell the vacation.

**Value enhancers** (they multiply the four core levers):
- **Scarcity** = limited units ("we only onboard N per month"). Must be true.
- **Urgency** = limited time (cohort start date, price rising, a bonus that expires). Must be true. False urgency and scarcity are easy to spot and destroy trust. Hormozi flags these as a fix he made in a portfolio company.
- **Bonuses**: stacked, named solutions to the next problems they'll hit.
- **Guarantees**: see section C.
- **Naming**: call the offer something that matches the avatar and the outcome.

**Offer building (trim and stack)** is for whoever designs the offer, but a closer should understand it. List every micro-problem the customer hits from purchase to result, write a solution for each, then keep the ones that are high-value to the customer and cheap to deliver, and cut the rest. On a call this becomes the stack: present those high-value pieces as specific solutions to the problems the prospect named.

**Offer price psychology:**
- "Buy one for $15, get two free" beats "three for $5 each". Free drives action even when the maths is the same.
- **Magnetic middle:** with three tiers, where you put the middle price decides what sells. Put the middle close to the top and the top looks like a steal. Spread them apart and the middle looks reasonable. Always have a very expensive option. It pushes buyers to the middle and catches the few who want the best.
- **Upsell sizing:** an upsell should be about 5x the core price, because the top 20% of customers have about 5x the spending power. A 10% upsell isn't worth asking for.
- **Raising prices on existing customers (script):** "Heads up, we're raising prices for new clients. Since you're an OG, I'll honour your old price if you lock in now. Otherwise it's the new price."
- **Commodity escape:** if prospects compare you on a standard unit (hourly rate, per-session), stop quoting in that unit. "We don't price by the hour, we price by the job." Hourly invites apples-to-apples shopping.

## B. Presenting the price (the diagnostic method)

Hormozi's diagnostic sale took a portfolio company's revenue per customer from $200 to $800 without changing the product, only how it was presented.

**Sequence:**
1. Pre-call/pre-sale questionnaire: the same "why are you here" asked several ways, including "if you had to quantify what this has cost you financially, what would it be?" and "if it continued five years, how much worse?" This raises their awareness of the pain.
2. Set up their account and get their payment details as standard procedure *before* anything is sold. (In person: "let me complete your profile." On Zoom: ask for full name/email/ID to "associate the right account". Adapt this to your company's compliance rules.)
3. Current state: they pick where they are ("the scale called you fat, not me". Let a third party, a chart or a number name it).
4. Desired state: they pick where they want to be. Because they set the goal, "the price came from them".
5. Obstacle: what's in between. Get their words so you can explain it back to them.
6. Present the vehicle (three pillars) and tie the price to the gap.
7. Offer an incentive to prepay.

**Worked example (Hormozi's own):** 200 lb → wants 140 lb = 60 lb. Safe pace 1.5 lb/week → 45 weeks. At $99/week that's about $4,500. Add a guarantee: show up to the workouts and log your food, and if you don't hit the weight we keep working with you for free until you do. Then the key line: *"So when you invest this $4,500, it means you can count that weight as gone for good. Put it out of your mind."* Then: "If I can save you a little money, do you want me to?" → prepay 20% off ($3,600) / half down 10% off ($4,050) / $99 a week.

**Generic template for any high-ticket offer:**
"You told me you're at [current] and want [desired] by [date]. The gap is [gap]. To close it you need [pillar A, B, C]. That's what the [program] does over [timeframe]. The full investment is [full price]. That means [desired outcome] stops being a someday thing. As long as you do [2–3 actions], we get you there."

**Bracing and anchoring:**
- "Okay, it is expensive." Say this before the number. A wealthy prospect pictures a bigger number than yours. A stretched prospect is braced. People take bad news better when they're braced for it.
- Going for the gasp: "When you say $100,000 first, a grand feels like a rounding error." If nobody gasps, the price (or the anchor) is too low.
- Present the full (payment-plan) price first and the discounts after. The opposite (low price, then "there's interest if you finance") makes the plan feel like a punishment. "Banks do it all the time and everyone hates banks."

**Tiers ("Maui"):** everyone gets to Maui, and the tiers are just how: swim (DIY), boat, commercial flight, private jet. "The likelihood of arrival is assumed. The variables are speed and quality of the journey." Start everyone at the top tier. If they say they're on a budget: "No worries, we can do the standard for you." Make sure they know the lower tier is *less for less*, not the same for less.

## C. Guarantees and risk reversal

Think of a guarantee as purchase insurance. You know more about delivery than they do ("you've done this 100 times, it's their first"), so you can carry more of the risk. It's a maths decision, not an emotional one. If an unconditional guarantee lifts sales 20% and refunds go from 1% to 5%, you're still well ahead.

**Four types:**
1. **Unconditional:** money back, no questions, for N days. The strongest. It turns the sale into a trial: "You can't make an informed decision until you're on the inside."
2. **Conditional:** "If you do X and Y and don't get Z by date, we'll [refund / pay you 2x / keep working free until you get it]." Build the conditions from what your best customers actually did (the fewest common actions). Each extra condition weakens the guarantee.
3. **Anti-guarantee:** lean into not having one. "If you need a guarantee, this isn't for you. I can't do the work for you. Does that sound fair?" Or, for proprietary know-how: "Once you've learned it you have it forever, so refunds don't make sense."
4. **Implied:** performance pricing: rev share, profit share, pay on results.

**Stacking:** "30-day unconditional, plus a 90-day conditional." The first gets them in, the second ties success to the behaviours that produce it.

**Wording rule:** never say "results guaranteed" (people tune it out). Spell it out: "If you don't lose six inches from your waist by [date] and you ask, I'll write you a cheque for the difference between what you paid and what you think it was worth."

**On a call:** the guarantee is what makes the best-case/worst-case close work, and it settles a lot of spouse hesitation ("sign up, and if your husband tells you he wants you to stay out of shape, have him call me").

## D. Payment options: step-downs

Order, presented one at a time, moving down only on a no:
1. **Prepay** (biggest incentive, e.g. 10–20% off). Say "prepayment discount", never "pay in full" or "PIF".
2. **Third-party financing** (there's a lender for nearly every legitimate service category, priced to the risk).
3. **Partial / half down** with a smaller discount.
4. **Payment plan** at the full-boat price. Match payments to when they get delivery.
5. **Continuity / smaller first step.** "Everyone buys something."

**Structure insights:**
- People drop off a payment plan far less often than they cancel a month-to-month subscription. A $300/month offer that churned at 3 months, re-sold as a "$10,000 program" with 36 months of financing, held customers for 8 months. Same product.
- Add an automatic renewal at the plan price when the program ends, and the plan becomes recurring revenue.
- Changing terms is fine (how much today, how it's spread). Changing price is not.

**Collecting payment:**
- Once they say yes, stop talking and take the details.
- Assumptive: "Do you want to use the card on file?" or "Chocolate or vanilla?" (He made his first supplement sale by asking *which*, not *whether*.)
- In person: ask for ID first, then "I'll trade you for whichever card you want to use". It kills "I left my wallet at home". If they say "my card isn't on me": "Pull up your banking app, there's an account number on the statement."
- Train collection first: "If they don't know how to collect payment, they have a 0% chance of closing." Hormozi trains sales "back to front": collection, then the ask, then the rest.
- Delayed close (when there's a decision maker or timing issue): "Let's get your card down. We'll run it Friday. If between now and then [partner] tells you they want you to keep struggling, I'll tear the contract up."

## E. Money objections: the reasoning, then the frames

**Know which layer you're on (Hormozi's "onion of blame").** Excuses peel from the outside in:
1. **Circumstances:** time, money, fit ("it's not a good time", "can't afford it", "not sure it's for me").
2. **Other people:** spouse, partner, boss.
3. **Self / avoidance:** "I need to think about it." Hearing this is *progress*: you're now talking to the person with the power to decide.

Each one is a distortion that hands their power to something outside them. Don't argue the surface. Peel back one layer and keep going.

**Is it even an objection?** "Wow, that's expensive" is often just a reaction. People have said it and bought plenty of times. Don't fire your closes at zombies that aren't there. Unless they say they don't want to buy, don't assume they don't.

**Separate price from belief:** "If it did exactly what we said, would it be worth it?" A yes means the issue isn't price, it's belief, and belief you handle with proof, a guarantee, and "don't let it burn you twice".

**The four money frames (use one to three, in any order):**
1. **"It's a lot for you, good."** "Is this a lot of money for you?" ... "That's actually what I want to hear. When it's a lot, people go all in. All our best stories come from people for whom it was a lot. If it were pocket change, I'd worry you wouldn't take it seriously."
2. **"It's not actually a lot" (relative value).** "If all this did was [outcome], would it be worth it?" Then compare: "A degree costs $50–200k and four years. This gets you [outcome] in a fraction of the time for a tenth of the price." Optionally the absurd version: "Would you believe me if I said it was a tenth of the price? No. So you know results like this cost something."
3. **"What's money good for anyway?" (pay with time or money).** "You'll spend this money in the next 12 months either way. Will it go on stuff that gets you nowhere, or on something that gets you somewhere? And you're buying these lessons either way, with money or with years of trial and error. We always spend what we value least. Which do you value more?"
4. **"Resourceful, not resources."** The Ferrari ("keys for $5k in 24 hours, you'd call ten friends"). The surprise tax bill everyone somehow covered. Shoe Dog's Phil Knight getting his vendors to cover payroll. Every self-made person started at zero, so the gap isn't resources, it's resourcefulness. Then help them find it: "Let's set a game plan together", which can include looking honestly at what they currently spend on (only once you have rapport).

**Supporting closes:**
- **Best case / worst case:** "Let's play it out. Best case, [life changed]. Worst case, [guarantee], you're where you were, and you were okay there. Both are risk-free. Walking out is the only option guaranteed not to get you there." (Hormozi: "I've probably closed more sales on best case worst case than any other close I have.")
- **Three options:** do it and get the result / don't do it and don't / do it and don't get it (covered by the guarantee). Only one of these is guaranteed to fail.
- **Cost of inaction:** "How much has *not* deciding cost you so far? Tired of another year of almost?"
- **The reason is the reason:** "The fact that you can't afford this is the very reason you need to do it. How long do you want 'I can't afford it' on your list of problems?"
- **When/then fallacy:** "I'll pay for the program that makes me money once I have money" is backwards. Put it next to "I'll start saving once I'm rich."
- **Closer or further:** "It doesn't have to be the perfect answer. Does this get you closer to [goal] than what you're doing now?"
- **Pre-ask money fit:** before the price, "If it's the right fit, do you have access to the funds, or know someone who does?" After the price, "do you have it or have access to it?" → "Then it's my obligation to help you get started."

**Negotiation:**
- "Can you do it for $X less?" → **"We could do it for more."** It re-anchors above, they feel they might lose the current price, and many just say "no, I'm good, I'll buy." Then offer terms, not price.
- **Cheap competitor close:** "If ours were the same price as theirs, which would you pick?" ... "Why?" (they list your advantages) ... "Right, and that's why we cost more." Usually gets a laugh. A comparison question is a buying signal.
- **Less for less:** "It's totally fair that they charge less. They don't do [the variables you raised]. If those don't matter to you, go with them. If they do, we're the only ones who do it."

## F. 3A reframing (use before any handle)

Before you answer *any* question or objection, especially price questions, run Hormozi's 3A:
1. **Acknowledge:** say it back. It builds rapport and buys you time to think.
2. **Associate:** tie it to how your best customers behave. "That's a great question. Our most successful clients usually ask that." The "step away" becomes a step towards buying, and they get a label to live up to (you can bring it back at the close: "people who think it through like you do tend to…").
3. **Ask:** a question about their question. "Which part specifically?" "What would make it a no?" "What are you most afraid of?" "What would it take for this to be a yes?"

Rules: whoever asks the questions controls the call. Nobody can disagree with a question ("be like smoke"). Never disagree with the prospect. For hard truths, use a straw man: "someone asked me this exact thing earlier today, can I share what I told them?", a past client ("you remind me of Sarah…"), or an outside authority ("my CEO told me something on this…").

Trap question on price ("what's included for that?" / "how many calls do I get?"): don't count features. Ask "What are you hoping to get out of [the calls]?" and sell the outcome.

## G. No-based and assumptive questions

- No-based questions feel safer to say yes to. "Would you be opposed to getting started today?" beats "Are you excited to move forward today?" People keep their sense of control by saying "no".
- Assume the next step: "Which start date works better, the 3rd or the 10th?" or "Card ending 4421?"

## H. Conviction and prep (what makes the price feel fair)

- Reread testimonials out loud every day. If the company has none, Hormozi's advice to the rep is to find another company.
- "Would you sell this to your mother?" Belief isn't yes or no, it's how much you believe.
- Expect no. "No is the job. If they were going to say yes, you wouldn't be needed."
- Prep means smooth: on any objection you should already be breathing in to start your answer. Drill 2–3 handles per objection type until you can say them without thinking. Never memorize closes word for word, understand the reasoning behind them.
- Train with low-qualification leads: "If I can close somebody with no money, no job, I've gotten really good."

## I. Reinforce the decision (after the money)

- The handshake, not the handoff: introduce the onboarding person by name, say what they'll help with, and share what you learned (goals, fears, pains).
- Book a meeting from a meeting: they always know who they'll talk to next and when.
- A personal welcome video or note. Prospects decide in the first 48 hours whether they believe in you, and that's where buyer's remorse and early refunds start.
