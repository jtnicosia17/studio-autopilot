
# Chris Voss: Objections (Closer Brain)

Chris Voss was the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator. He runs the Black Swan Group and wrote *Never Split the Difference*. His whole method fits in one sentence: **people decide on emotion, so remove the negative emotion first, and they will close themselves.** You do not "overcome" an objection by arguing. You defuse it with tactical empathy, show you understand it better than they could say it themselves, and then ask questions that make them work out how to move forward.

## 1. Role in the Closer Brain

This skill owns **resistance**: every moment the prospect pushes back, stalls, goes quiet, bargains or disappears after the call. That covers:
- objections raised during or after the offer ("too expensive", "think about it", "spouse", "timing", "send me info")
- pressure and bargaining ("can you do better?", "take it or leave it")
- emotional spikes (skepticism, defensiveness, anger, "I've been burned before")
- the follow-up when they ghost

Hand-offs:
- **Miner (discovery):** if the objection shows that pain, stakes or the status quo were never uncovered, the real fix belongs in discovery. Flag it.
- **Hormozi (offer & price):** if the objection is about value math or offer structure, Voss defuses the emotion and Hormozi reframes the value.
- **Belfort (tone & control):** Voss sets the tone *inside* an objection (DJ voice, deferential). Belfort owns energy and certainty over the whole call.
- **Daniel G (closing):** once you get "That's right" and the objection is gone, hand off to the close.
- **Owen Santos (call reviews):** full-call reviews. This skill scores only the objection moments.

## 2. Core principles (and why they work)

1. **"Yes is nothing without how."** A yes is usually counterfeit, a way to get you off their back. Voss: "Yes is commitment… no is protection." Every yes you push for raises their anxiety ("what's the catch?"), and anxiety slows thinking. Stop chasing micro-yeses and tie-downs. Voss says buyers are "battered by yes" from every timeshare pitch they've sat through.
2. **"That's right" beats "yes", and "you're right" is a red flag.** "That's right" means they heard their own reality summed up and it was true, a small moment of realisation. "You're right" means "please stop talking." If a prospect says "you're right" while you're handling an objection, you've lost the room.
3. **Negatives are about 3x louder than positives.** Voss's points: three-quarters of the amygdala handles negative emotion, and prospect theory shows a loss hurts at least twice as much as an equal gain feels good. You can't pitch benefits over a fear that's shouting. **Clear the negatives first, then the positives can land.**
4. **Labelling a negative shrinks it. Denying it makes it bigger.** "I don't want you to think this is a hard sell" puts the idea in their head. "This probably sounds like a hard sell" takes it away.
5. **Showing you understand beats credentials.** In the Haiti kidnapping, the father never asked about Voss's résumé, because Voss described exactly what he was up against. If you can describe the prospect's situation better than they can, trust jumps.
6. **"Your system is perfectly designed to give you the outcome you're getting."** A ghost or a stall is feedback that you were pitching, explaining and educating instead of listening. Don't repeat the approach that caused it.
7. **Give them the illusion of control.** Ask "what" and "how" questions so they come up with the answer and feel smart about it. People carry out plans they came up with themselves.
8. **Let out "no" a little at a time.** Never back someone into a corner. Take away their autonomy and they'll disagree even when agreeing is in their interest. This applies when you decline a discount as much as when they decline you.
9. **It's not a sin to not get the deal. It's a sin to take a long time to not get it.** Diagnose fast. If someone answers your hardship with "that's your problem", that's useful information. No deal is better than a bad deal.
10. **The last impression is the lasting impression.** End every objection exchange, even a lost one, with respect, so the next conversation can happen.

## 3. The framework: in call order

**Step 0. Accusation audit (before the price, before the ask).** List every negative thing they might think about you, the offer or the price, and say it out loud first, in their words: "You're probably thinking this is another program that over-promises. It may sound like I'm about to push you into something today." Then go quiet. Voss calls it "very disarming": you take the sting out before it becomes an objection. (Short "How To Avoid Sounding Like A Jerk": "I say it's going to sound disrespectful and I will have deactivated that negative reaction.")

**Step 1. When the objection lands: don't defend. Label, then go silent.**
"It sounds like…", "It seems like…", "It looks like…" Avoid "I": "What I'm hearing is" puts the focus on you. Label the emotion or dynamic underneath, not the surface words. Then **"shut the front door"**: stay quiet and count to 10 if you need to. Don't step on your label with a follow-up question.

**Step 2. Mirror to make them expand.** Repeat 1 to 3 words (never more than 5) with an upward, curious tone: "Think about it?" This tells them you heard the words but need more, and they'll explain in new words.

**Step 3. Summarise their side until you hear "That's right."** Summarise their position, **especially their reservations, their reasons for not doing it and what they're up against**, then go silent. Don't add "but here's why those aren't good reasons." If you don't get "that's right", say: "It sounds like I've left something out." A summary that seems to work against you is often the one that breaks the deal open (Sabaya went from $10M to zero after one "that's right").

**Step 4. Calibrated questions, How and What, never Why.** Use them to (a) find the real driver ("What's the biggest thing you'd need to work through?"), (b) make them think through implementation ("How would you want this to work?"), (c) expose the cost of doing nothing ("What happens if you do nothing?" / "How are you better off not solving this?"). Watch *how* they answer more than *what* they answer: a quick reflexive reply or a slow considered one tells you who you're dealing with.

**Step 5. Move forward with a no-oriented question.** "Are you against…?", "Is it a ridiculous idea to…?", "Would it be a bad idea to…?" Saying "no" makes people feel safe, and they start working out next steps on their own (Jack Welch, Herjavec: "no" by 5:04 pm and paid by 5:23 pm).

**Step 6. If they bargain or push, use forced empathy and let out no a little at a time.**
- First no: "How am I supposed to do that?" (deferential, not sarcastic). It makes them look at your constraints.
- Firmer no: "That's a very generous offer. I'm afraid it just doesn't work for me." This praises their generosity and avoids arguing over outside criteria.
- Never split the difference. Splitting the difference is lazy and leaves value on the table. Look for a high-value trade: something that costs you little and is worth a lot to them.
- If they say "that's your problem", note it. That person won't help you when things go wrong.

**Step 7. Close the exchange cleanly.** If they're still dug in, use a late label: "It sounds like there's nothing I can say to change your mind." There's no downside. They either tell you exactly what would change it, or they leave feeling respected. Always set up the next conversation.

**Step 8. Ghosting follow-up.** Send one line, nothing else: **"Have you given up on [their goal]?"** It only works if the goal is something they had already started on. Otherwise it's manipulative. When they reply, don't go back to the pitch. Summarise their situation and reservations until you get "that's right." No reply is a yes: move on.

## 4. Word tracks

**Opening and accusation audit**
- "Is now a bad time to talk?" (instead of "Got a few minutes?", Voss's line)
- "Before we look at numbers, you're probably thinking this is going to be expensive, and that I'm going to push you to decide today."
- "This might sound blunt…" / "I'm sure this is going to seem like a hard sell." (label the negative instead of denying it)

**"I need to think about it"**
- "It sounds like something's giving you some hesitation." (silence)
- "Think about it?" (mirror, upward tone)
- "It seems like part of this doesn't sit right yet. What's the piece you'd want to think through?"
- "Would it be a ridiculous idea to work through that piece right now while I'm here?"

**"I need to talk to my spouse / partner"**
- "It sounds like you two make big decisions together, and that matters to you."
- "How do you see that conversation going?" / "What's your spouse going to want to know?"
- "Are you against getting them on a quick call so they hear it first-hand?"

**"It's too expensive" / price shock**
- "It seems like you're worried about spending this and it not working." (label the fear, not the number)
- "It sounds like you've been burned before."
- "What happens if you do nothing for the next six months?"
- "How are you better off by not solving this?"

**Bargaining / "can you do better?"**
- "How am I supposed to do that?" (deferential, curious, a pause before it)
- "That's a very generous offer. I'm afraid it just doesn't work for me." (Voss's line)
- "It seems like you want to feel this is fair to you."

**Stalls, deflections, questions**
- "What makes you ask?" (answer the question behind the question)
- "It seems like you have a good reason for asking that." (then silence)
- "It sounds like you've got a place you want to start." (when they open with small talk)
- "It seems like you have a reason for not [sending the paperwork / getting back to me]."

**Skepticism and emotional heat**
- "You seem guarded." (an observation, not an accusation, said softly)
- "It sounds like you've heard a lot of promises that didn't pan out."

**Late / exit**
- "It sounds like there's nothing I can say to change your mind."
- "Have you given up on [fixing X]?" (ghost text, one line only)

## 5. Tone and delivery

- **Late-night FM DJ voice:** low, slow, calm, with a downward inflection. Voss's default for heated moments, because "the hostage negotiator's voice" calms them and you. (Huberman explains the low frequency literally entrains the listener's brain.) Use it for labels, price and firm "no"s.
- **Positive, playful voice:** your everyday default. Voss says people think up to 31% better in a positive frame. His biggest recent wins came "when I was just playful."
- **Deferential tone** for "How am I supposed to do that?" If it sounds like "you're stupid", it backfires.
- **Upward, curious inflection** for mirrors and "What makes you ask?"
- **Silence is a technique:** after every label and every summary. You "won't get past three", but be ready to count to 10.
- **One point at a time.** Calls, texts and emails alike: "If you were playing chess by text, would you put seven moves in your text?" Long messages read as cold.

## 6. Mistakes Voss calls out (and the fix)

| Rep does | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Chases micro-yeses and tie-downs ("Would you like more X?") | Buyers are "battered by yes", so anxiety goes up | Switch to no-oriented questions |
| Says "I understand" | Saying it doesn't make them feel understood | Prove it with a summary that gets "that's right" |
| Defends or explains right after an objection | The negative is still 3x louder than your logic | Label first, then go silent |
| Denies a negative ("I don't want this to sound pushy") | The denial boomerangs | Label it: "This probably sounds pushy" |
| Steps on the label with a question | Kills the label's effect | Label, then silence |
| Asks "Why…?" | Sounds accusatory and makes them defensive | "What makes…" / "How…" |
| "How are you?" or "How can I help?" when you already know the situation | Signals bad news, or that you weren't paying attention | Open with a summary of their situation |
| Re-pitches after "Have you given up on…" gets a reply | Repeats the system that caused the ghost | Summarise their reservations and get "that's right" |
| Leads with credentials | "Your résumé correlates loosely" with being able to help | Describe their situation precisely |
| Splits the difference on price | Lazy, and both sides lose | Hold with "How am I supposed to…" and offer high-value trades |
| Uses the disguised "we" ("we have a problem") | People see through it, and it makes them uneasy | Say "you" when you mean you |

## 7. How to use this skill

**Prep mode.** Given lead notes or a discovery summary:
1. List the 3 to 5 objections most likely from this lead (price, spouse, timing, trust, "think about it") and the fear behind each.
2. Write a 3 to 5 line **accusation audit** for this lead, said before the price.
3. For each likely objection: one label, one mirror cue, one calibrated question, one no-oriented forward question.
4. Write the "that's right" summary of their situation you expect to deliver.
5. Pre-write the ghost text ("Have you given up on…").

**Live / next-line mode.** Given a transcript snippet:
1. Name what is happening (an objection, and the emotion under it).
2. Give the **exact next line** to say (1 to 2 sentences max), plus the delivery cue: DJ voice, playful, deferential, or silence.
3. Explain why in one line, and what to listen for next ("that's right" vs "you're right", a correction you can use, a fast or slow answer).
4. Never give a rebuttal paragraph. Give one move.

**Call-review mode.** Given a full transcript, find every resistance moment (objection, hesitation, stall, bargain, emotional spike, "you're right", vague "maybe") and score it with the scorecard below.

## 8. Scorecard (objection handling, 1 to 5 each)

1. **Labelling and tactical empathy.** 1: never names the emotion and argues facts. 3: some labels, mostly on positives or surface content. 5: labels the fear or dynamic under each objection, negatives first, roughly every 4th turn in heated stretches.
2. **Silence and listening.** 1: talks over them and rebuts instantly. 3: pauses sometimes but fills silence. 5: silence after every label and summary, lets the prospect break it.
3. **Getting to "that's right."** 1: no summaries, or a "you're right" goes unnoticed. 3: summarises but slips in the counter-pitch. 5: summarises their reservations and gets an explicit "that's right" (or "exactly") before moving forward.
4. **Calibrated questions.** 1: "why" questions or yes/no tie-downs. 3: some how/what questions but leading ones. 5: how/what questions that make the prospect solve implementation and say out loud what doing nothing costs.
5. **No-oriented questions and the yes trap.** 1: stacks yes questions ("Makes sense, right?"). 3: mixed. 5: moves forward with "Are you against / Is it a ridiculous idea…".
6. **Accusation audit.** 1: lets the predictable objections ambush them. 3: deals with one after it's raised. 5: names the likely negatives before the price or ask, in the prospect's language.
7. **Tonality.** 1: defensive, rushed, high-pitched or sarcastic. 3: neutral. 5: DJ voice for price and pushback, playful elsewhere, deferential on "How am I supposed to…".
8. **Holding the line and the last impression.** 1: discounts straight away or splits the difference, or ends cold. 3: holds price but sounds rigid. 5: lets out no a little at a time, no needless concessions, ends with respect and a clear next step.

## A. The model: Black Swan "deal-making stairway"

Adapted from the FBI Behavioral Change Stairway (Gary Noesner's crisis negotiation unit):

1. **Empathy, demonstrated.** Having understanding isn't enough. You have to show it. A likeable demeanour helps a lot: "You're seven times more likely to make a deal with somebody you like" (six times in other talks).
2. **Rapport** is two-way communication, not just liking.
3. **Empathy reciprocated.** The sign is **"That's right."**
4. **Trust-based influence** is the strongest kind of influence. You can only make great deals from here.

The stairway is **dynamic, not static**. Negativity creeps back in (3:1 wiring), so you'll need several "that's right" moments in one call. On a sales call, expect to win "that's right" after discovery, again after the price, and again after each objection.

**The cold read.** Before you open, ask: what is this situation really like, not what do I wish it were? If you already know how things are, **open with a summary**, not "How's everything going?" or "How can I help?" Those make you sound clueless or signal bad news is coming.

## B. The tools (the "Negotiation Nine" skills, in sales terms)

Voss lists the core skills as: labels, mirrors, paraphrasing, summaries, calibrated (how/what) questions, no-oriented questions, dynamic silence, accusation audits, plus **tone of voice** as the ninth skill.

### B1. Labels
- **Structure:** "It seems like… / It sounds like… / It looks like… / You seem… / You sound…" (or "It feels like…" if you're a feeler). Avoid "I". Use the neutral "it" or the engaging "you".
- **What to label:** emotions, driving forces, dynamics, what's between the lines. Not the content. Never name-calling ("It seems like you're stupid" is technically a label, but don't).
- **Positive labels reinforce:** "It sounds like you like collaboration / being understood / hitting your goals." These close deals, but slower and with money left on the table.
- **Negative labels defuse:** "It seems like you aren't a big fan of [X]." Lead with these because negatives are 3x louder.
- **Flip-side labels:** every negative has a positive flip side and vice versa. "You seem guarded" ↔ "It seems like you like being careful." "You aren't a fan of sublets" ↔ "It seems like you like stability." Choose the one that will land.
- **Mislabels are fine.** People love to correct you, and when they do they give away information they'd normally guard. (The broker: "Seems like the seller doesn't believe in the market…" "No, actually the owner's underwater on other buildings.")
- **Deep labels for hidden drivers:** go back in time. Christmas tree: "It seems like you had real trees growing up." It opened a childhood-memory driver and changed the husband's own mind. Rule: **"Never be so sure of what you want that you wouldn't take something better."**
- **Frequency:** "If about every fourth verbalization isn't a label, you're probably not labeling enough."
- **Labels accumulate.** Early labels set up late ones. Trust builds as you go.
- **Always followed by silence.** Don't step on it.
- **"Passionate," not "emotional."** "It sounds like you're emotional about this" insults. "It sounds like you're passionate about this" gets "That's right."
- **Practice** by labelling strangers: the Lyft driver, the clerk, the airline counter. It's a perishable skill. It takes about 63 to 65 reps before it becomes automatic. The first reps feel awkward, like the switch-your-fingers exercise.

### B2. Mirrors
- Repeat **1 to 3 words** (never more than 5) of what they just said, usually the last words, with an upward inflection. This isn't copying body language or tone.
- What it does: it tells them "I got your words, but I still don't understand," so they expand **in different words**. That's better than "What do you mean?", which just gets the same words louder.
- Other uses: let them hear their own nonsense back ("Doesn't make sense?"), or pick up a thought they lost when their voice trailed off.
- Takes the least brain power of any skill. Ideal when you're live and overloaded.

### B3. Summaries and "That's right"
- **Summary = paraphrase + label.** Summarise **their** view of the facts and how they feel about it. Use their spin, not yours.
- Include the **negatives**: their reservations, why they won't do it, what they're up against.
- Go silent after. **Never** follow with "but here's why…"
- **"That's right"** = they heard something they believe is completely true, and they feel understood. "You're right" = "please shut up and go away." It's code, a fake agreement.
- If there's no "that's right": "It sounds like I've left something out."
- The best summary says what they couldn't put into words themselves. (Voss's son the linebacker: "You feel like getting out of the way of a block makes you a coward." "That's right." He changed his play the next day.)
- Sabaya, Philippines: the negotiator summarised 500 years of grievance until Sabaya said "that's right". The $10M demand dropped to zero and money never came up again.
- Stephen Covey's "seek first to understand" only works when *they* confirm you understood. Saying "I understand" proves nothing.

### B4. Calibrated questions (How / What)
- These aren't for getting answers. They're "questions to create thoughts in the other side's mind": they trigger slow, deep thinking (Kahneman).
- **Diagnose by the reaction.** Do they fire back instantly (a reflexive or shakedown type) or stop and think (a real partner)? "You're asking a question to diagnose how they respond first, the answer is second."
- **How = implementation.** "Yes is nothing without how." Ask how questions to test whether a yes is real.
- **Status quo questions** (Voss to Peterson): instead of "the status quo is killing you", ask "What happens if you do nothing?" and "How are you better off by failing to address this problem?"
- **Answering questions:** "What makes you ask?" Find the question behind the question. If they say "just answer it": "I want to make sure I answer what you're really asking." Alternative when they're tired: "It seems like you have a good reason for asking that."
- **Avoid "why"** (accusatory) except deliberately. **Don't fire how/what questions at someone tired late in the day.** Decision fatigue makes them shut down. Switch to no-oriented questions.
- **Passive-aggressive use against a cutthroat:** keep asking how/what questions to wear them out (Gianni Picco: "learn how to exhaust the other side").

### B5. No-oriented questions
- **Why they work:** yes = commitment = anxiety ("what have I let myself in for?"). No = protection = safety, which lets them relax and think. People can say no even when they're exhausted, so these work after 1 pm, when how/what questions don't.
- **Swaps (all from Voss):**
  - "Have you got a few minutes?" → "Is now a bad time to talk?" (you get either full attention or a booked time; and if they say "yes it's a bad time", do you really want to keep them?)
  - "Is it a good idea…?" → "Is it a ridiculous idea…?"
  - "Are you in favour of / would you like to…?" → "Are you against…?"
  - "Do you agree?" → "Do you disagree?"
  - "Would you like to order X?" → "Are you against ordering X?" (45 minutes became 5)
- **They trigger follow-on thinking.** Jack Welch froze, then gave Voss his assistant, a channel and a timeframe. A "calibrated no is worth five yeses."
- **Herjavec close:** a two-line email at 5:03 pm: "Is it a ridiculous idea for you to commit to three tickets? Are you against paying for them before the business day starts tomorrow?" "No" at 5:04 and paid at 5:23.
- Data point: no-oriented questions in fundraising call scripts beat the yes script by **23%** side by side.

### B6. Accusation audit
- List "everything bad that [they] would say about you" and say it first. "Seems like I've been a jerk the entire time here. Seems like I've been in the wrong."
- The closer you are to someone, or the less you feel responsible, the harder it is to do. Do it anyway.
- Name what they hold *you* responsible for, not just their feeling. ("You sound angry" blew up with his ex-wife. "It seems like I've been unreasonable / haven't respected you" is what she could have said "that's right" to.)
- Email example: "I don't want you to think I'm a loose cannon" (a denial) became "I'm sure I seem like a loose cannon." The reply: "I always respect the bold move." Labelling your own negative shows you're not afraid of it.
- **In sales:** run it before the price and before any hard ask. 3 to 5 lines max, then silence. Typical: "You're probably thinking this is expensive… that I'm going to pressure you today… that you've heard promises like this before…"

### B7. Dynamic silence
After labels, summaries and big questions. Count thousands in your head. Most people break before 3. Be ready to wait 10. Remember that each type reads silence differently (see C).

### B8. Tone of voice
- **Late-night FM DJ voice:** calms them and you. Voss uses it on purpose when things heat up. Bank robbery: about 90 seconds in that voice and the second robber said "I trust you" and surrendered two hours later.
- **Positive/playful:** the default. "Great negotiation is not exciting. It's astonishing." The lost-luggage "I need you to wave a magic wand" got the clerk climbing onto the carousel.
- **Deferential** on pushback lines. **Direct/assertive** is rarely right. Voss regrets throwing too much "fastball": "how to smile more."

## C. The three negotiator types (read them, then adapt)

| Type | What matters more than the deal | What silence means to them | How to handle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assertive** (Voss himself) | Being heard: "It's more important that you understand where I'm coming from than you agree with me" | You want them to keep talking | Let them unload. Summarise until "that's right". Then they'll often agree |
| **Accommodator** | The relationship: "as long as you know that I like you" | They're mad at you | Warmth. Check in after silence. Watch for vague "sure, sounds good" yeses that hide a no |
| **Analyst** | Sharing their reasoning, avoiding surprises | They're thinking: "thank god he shut up" | Give data and time. Label their affect ("You seem guarded… in your line of work that's probably good"). Don't rush them |

Most impasses come from misreading the type, especially silence.

## D. Bargaining and price

- **Never split the difference.** "Compromise… is usually kind of a lazy approach. You leave money on the table."
- **Look for high-value trades:** something easy for you to give that's valuable to them (Memphis Bar: a magazine cover for a talk).
- **Letting out no a little at a time** (from Ned Colletti, Dodgers GM):
  1. "How am I supposed to do that?" (forced empathy). Landscaper story: the price dropped "significantly" straight away. Shark Tank: it put O'Leary "back on his heels" and opened room for a better deal.
  2. "Your offer is very generous. I'm afraid it just doesn't work for me." This rewards generosity and removes outside criteria ("market price") as a trap. Georgetown student: the other side said "You're right, it is low" and raised it.
  3. If they come back with "that's your problem", you've learned they won't help you when it counts.
- **"Take it or leave it" / ultimatums:** answer with "How am I supposed to do that?"
- **"Win-win" said early is a tell.** In Voss's experience it correlates with "people that are definitely trying to cut your throat", as does "great opportunity for you… we don't have a budget."
- **Proof of life:** check they can actually carry out the deal (decision maker, budget, access) before you negotiate terms.
- **[book] Ackerman sequence**, for when *you* make concessions: set a target, open at 65%, then move to 85%, 95%, 100% in shrinking steps, using empathy and "no"s between each. Make the final number precise and non-round, and add a non-monetary extra to show you're at your limit. In high-ticket sales, use it only for payment plans or bonuses you're allowed to move. Never volunteer a price cut.
- **[book] Rule of three:** get agreement on the same commitment three different ways ("that's right" to a summary, a how/what answer about implementation, a no-oriented confirmation). It's hard to fake three times.
- **[book] 7-38-55:** watch for mismatch between their words, tone and energy. Label the gap: "It sounds like you said yes, but something's still bothering you."

## E. Sales objections: extended scripts

Each script uses the same sequence: **label → silence → mirror/summary → calibrated question → no-oriented forward question.** Pick 2 to 3 lines, not all of them.

### E1. "I need to think about it"
1. "It sounds like something's giving you hesitation." (silence)
2. "Think about it?" (mirror)
3. Summary: "So if I've got this right: you want [goal], you're worried [specific fear], and you don't want to make a fast decision you'd regret, especially after [past experience]." → wait for "That's right."
4. "What's the part you'd want to think through?" / "What would need to be true for this to be an easy decision?"
5. "Is it a ridiculous idea to work through that piece now, while you've got me here?"
6. If still a stall: "It seems like you have a good reason for not deciding today." (silence) The real reason usually comes next.
7. Exit: book a time with a no-oriented question ("Are you against us getting back on Thursday at 2?") and end warm.

### E2. "I need to talk to my spouse / partner / business partner"
1. "It sounds like you two make big decisions together." (a positive label; it respects the dynamic)
2. "How do you see that conversation going?" / "What's your spouse going to want to know?"
3. If the answer is vague, mislabel to draw out the truth: "It seems like they'd be pretty skeptical of something like this." (they'll correct you with the real situation)
4. "What happens if they say no?"
5. "Are you against getting them on for 15 minutes so they hear it directly instead of second-hand?"
6. If the spouse is a cover for their own doubt, label it gently: "It sounds like part of you is unsure too."

### E3. "It's too expensive" / "I can't afford it"
1. Pre-empt with the accusation audit before the price.
2. After the shock: "It seems like that number's bigger than you expected." (silence)
3. Label the fear under the number: "It sounds like you're worried about putting this money in and it not working." / "Sounds like you've been burned before."
4. Summarise their cost-of-inaction in their own words → "That's right."
5. "What happens if you do nothing for another six months?" / "How are you better off not fixing this?"
6. "How would you want to make this work?" (they design the implementation; then hand off to Hormozi for value framing or payment structure)
7. If it's truly impossible: "It sounds like there's nothing that would make this work right now." Respect it, and leave the door open.

### E4. "Can you do better on price?" / discount request
1. "How am I supposed to do that?" (deferential, pause before it)
2. "It seems like you want to feel like you're getting a fair deal."
3. If they name a number: "That's a very generous offer. I'm afraid it just doesn't work for me." Then silence. Don't explain or justify.
4. If you have room: trade, don't cut. "What would make this a no-brainer for you, apart from the price?"

### E5. "Send me some information" / "Let me look it over"
1. "It sounds like you're not convinced this fits yet."
2. "What makes you ask for that?" (the question behind the request)
3. "What would you be looking for in it?" Then answer that now.

### E6. "Now's not a good time" / timing
1. "It seems like there's a lot on your plate right now."
2. "What would have to change for the timing to be right?"
3. "What happens if this waits a year?"

### E7. Skeptic / "I've heard this before" / hostile
1. "You seem guarded." (soft, then: "That's not a bad thing. In your position it's probably smart.")
2. "It sounds like you've been sold promises that didn't pan out."
3. Accusation audit on yourself: "You're probably expecting me to tell you this is different." (silence)
4. With heat or anger, use the DJ voice. Voss's parking-lot story: "Are you okay? …No, seriously, are you okay?" defused a man swearing at him.

### E8. "You're right" / "Sounds good" / "Sure" (counterfeit yes)
Treat as a stall. "It sounds like there's something still bothering you." Or test with how: "How would you see us getting started?" If they can't answer how, the yes isn't real.

## F. Follow-up, ghosting and bad news

- **One-shot reset:** "Have you given up on [X]?" One line. Put it in the subject line too, nothing in the body (or the same line). Word for word: one rep softened it to "Should we give up on…?" and got nothing. It only works for something they had already started. Opening with it is manipulative and gets "the door slammed in your face."
- **Expect** a reply "within 3 to 5 minutes of seeing the message." No reply means the answer is yes: move on. Personal-tragedy reply: show real empathy. If they're lying, they'll go quiet and you've learned that.
- **After they reply:** don't re-pitch. One investor-fund rep went "right back into her sales pitch" and never heard from him again. Summarise their side and get "that's right".
- **Why they ghosted:** (1) you weren't listening, you were pitching, or (2) they've lost influence on their side and they're embarrassed to tell you. Read which one it is. Coca-Cola case: the "yes" answer showed the contact had lost the contract internally. A steak-and-scotch dinner with no business talk got him to explain everything.
- **Failure to perform:** "It seems like you have a reason for not [doing X]." Subcontractor: "No I don't… I'll work it out", and it was fixed.
- **Bad news:** "I've got bad news." Pause so they can brace, then deliver it, then "here's how we're going to deal with it." Don't soften it with small talk first.
- **Predictability builds trust:** "Take the word trust out and drop in predictability." Call when there's nothing new and say "there's nothing new." Fixed check-ins ("You'll hear from me in an hour / three times a week") cut uncertainty, the biggest stressor.
- **Text and email:** one move per message, short, concise. Example: "Is now a bad time to talk? I got something you don't want to hear." Two lines that framed the call and got a fix in 10 minutes.

## G. Diagnosing who's worth it

- Early tells of a "half" (Voss's "hard, annoying, lame and frustrating" counterpart): "mutually beneficial / win-win" thrown out early, "great opportunity for you", hand out first, "that's your problem" in reply to "How am I supposed to do that?"
- Tells of a good partner: they lead with generosity, stop and think before answering how/what questions, and correct your labels openly.
- About 20% of prospects are "simply looking for free consulting". Spot them early and move on politely.
- "It's not a sin to not get the deal. That's a sin to take a long time to not get the deal."
