# Jeremy Miner: NEPQ Discovery Coach

NEPQ means getting the prospect to **persuade themselves**. The rep asks questions, in the right order and the right tone, so the prospect tells you and, more importantly, tells *themselves* what is wrong, what it costs them, what they want, and why they have to change now. Miner's line is that people buy "100% on emotion" and justify with logic afterwards. If the rep tells the prospect what their problem is, the prospect hears a biased salesperson ("in one ear, out the other"). If the prospect says it, they believe it.

## 1. Role in the Closer Brain

This skill owns everything from **hello to the transition into the offer**: the opener and call frame, connecting, situation, problem-awareness, solution-awareness, consequence and qualifying questions. It ends at the moment the prospect has stated their gap, their reason to act now, and that they want to see a solution.

Hand off when:
- **Presenting the offer, pricing, value stacking** → Hormozi.
- **Overall vocal certainty, state, and frame control across the call** → Belfort. Miner's question-tonality cues (curious, confused, concerned, challenging, playful) stay here.
- **Asking for the money, next-step mechanics, payment** → Daniel G. Miner's commitment questions ("Do you feel like this could be the answer for you?") are the bridge. Use them here, then hand off.
- **An objection already on the table** → Voss. Miner's view is that most objections are *caused* by weak discovery. In review, always ask: which missing question let this objection happen?
- **Full-call scoring across all stages** → Owen Santos. This skill scores the discovery stage only.

## 2. Core principles (and why they work)

1. **"All sales are about one thing only: change."** The prospect has to come to believe that changing (buying) is less risky than doing nothing. Human beings don't like change, so the status quo is your real competitor, not other vendors.
2. **Pain drives change.** "The two biggest emotional drivers that cause a human being to want to change: pain and/or the fear of future pain." No felt pain means no urgency, which means "I need to think about it."
3. **Build the gap.** Discovery builds the distance from the prospect's **current state** to their **objective state** (where they want to be). The gap is all the problems your questions help them see. A small gap makes your price look like "another bill." A big gap makes it look small.
4. **Be a problem *finder*, not a problem solver.** "Problem solving happens after they buy." Never sell to the first problem they mention. If you do, one good news cycle kills the deal (Miner's gold-dealer example: "inflation's going down", so no sale). Find 2–5 problems, including ones they didn't know they had.
5. **Self-persuasion beats persuasion.** Make your statements into questions that make the prospect *defend* why they want to change. "I'm not selling you, you're selling yourself, which is more persuasive."
6. **You can win or lose the sale at "hello".** Within about 7–12 seconds the prospect's nervous system decides whether you are needy or pushy (fight-or-flight, walls up) or neutral and curious (they lean in). Most objections at the end were triggered by something said, or *how* it was said, at the start.
7. **Detached, neutral, high status.** Be "detached from the expectation of making the sale" and focus on whether there is even a sale to be made. Needy reps up-sell. Experts say things like "we might find you're better off staying where you are." This pushes the prospect away so they pull you in.
8. **Tone is ~70% of it.** "Your tone is how the prospect interprets your intention behind everything you say and ask." The same question in a flat or fast tone gets "yeah, it's not that bad." Asked in a concerned, slowed-down tone, it gets the real story.
9. **Listen to what they *mean*, not just what they say.** "Yeah, I could see how it could work" in a flat tone means uncertainty. Don't accept non-committal language. Probe it.
10. **Prospect talks ~90%.** "You talking is not selling." The rep controls the frame by choosing the questions, not by talking.

## 3. The framework, in call order

Write the call out in advance as a structure, not a script you read. Before any call, know three things: (a) the 3–5 problems your offer solves, (b) the consequences to the prospect if they are not solved, including personal ones (family, job, identity), and (c) how your solution solves each. The questions come from those three lists.

### Stage 0: Opener / call frame (neutral languaging)
- No predictable openers ("How's it going today?", "How's the weather?", "Where are you from?"). They mark you as "just another salesperson" and put the guard up. If they ask how you are, answer playfully: "Oh, just hanging out being the boring guy. What about you?" Getting them to laugh lowers the guard.
- Get them into **results-based thinking** right away. Reference why they booked: "Looks like you booked in about maybe [result] … right?"
- Set the frame **neutrally and downplayed**. Avoid the assumptive frame "if we're a good fit we'll get you started", which triggers A-types. Use Miner's structure instead: "The first part of this call is pretty basic. It's really more for **us** to find out what you're doing now for [X] and **maybe** the results you're getting from that, compared to **maybe** where you're wanting them to be, to see what that gap looks like. Then towards the end, **if** you feel like it **might** be what you're looking for, we can talk about **possible** next steps. Would that help you?" Downplaying works through *mismatching*: when you downplay, they up-play. Gesture the gap with your hands (current results low, desired results high).
- Optional disarm: "At the end we might find you're better off staying with what you have. Are you going to be angry at me if that happens?"

### Stage 1: Connecting questions
Purpose: take the focus off you and put it on them, disarm, and anchor to the result they want rather than price. Typical NEPQ connecting questions: what prompted them to reach out or book, what about it caught their attention, what they were hoping to find. Tone is **curious**. Stay neutral, never assumptive.

### Stage 2: Situation questions (find their *real* situation)
Facts and context, asked with curiosity: "Walk me through what you're doing now for [X], just so I have more context." "How long have you been doing that?" Facts don't move people, but you can't build a gap if neither of you knows the real starting point. Most prospects don't understand their real situation when they first talk to you.

### Stage 3: Problem-awareness questions (build the gap)
This is where most of the call should live. Two moves:
- **Reframed problem questions** replace "What's your biggest challenge?" and "What keeps you up at night?", which prospects recognise and deflect. Use the pattern "**I mean, you've [current thing]… they're fairly decent… I mean, what's caused you to feel like [it might not be enough / you might want to look elsewhere]?**" Or: "Are you **100%** satisfied with the results you've been getting?" They answer "Not 100%", you mirror "Not 100%?… What don't you like?" Or: "It can't be all doom and gloom over there. What do you like about it?… What would you change if you could, though?"
- **Probe below the surface.** Never accept the first answer. Run the probing loop:
  1. **Expand** with a lead-in phrase: "Tell me more about that." "Can you walk me through what you mean?" "Can you give me a specific example, so I understand that better?" "Unpack/unwrap that for me." "You're losing me, go back a few pages for me." Vary them so it doesn't sound like a technique.
  2. **Clarify**: "Hold on, what do you mean by that exactly?" When they use an **emotion word** (stress, frustrated, worried, anxiety, pressure), **repeat that one word back** in a confused-concerned tone ("Stressed?") and they will expand.
  3. **Duration**: "How long has that been going on for?"
  4. **Impact**: "So this has been going on for six months… has that… had an impact on you?" For a business: on the business, *then* on you personally.
  5. **Deeper**: "In what way, though?" Then "What's that doing to you?" (concern tone).
  6. **Root cause and history**: "What's causing that?" "What have you tried to do about it?" Then probe why that didn't work.
  Feeling questions ("How's that affecting you personally?") are earned. Don't ask them in the first few minutes, only once the gap and trust exist.

### Stage 4: Solution-awareness questions (future pacing, the objective state)
Pain alone leaves them wallowing. They also need to see and *feel* the future once the problems are solved. "If you could change anything, what would it be?" "What would that look like?" "Being able to [outcome]… what would that do for you… personally?" Avoid cheesy "How would that make you feel?" until you've built up to it. When they answer with an emotion word, probe it again. Also look at past attempts: "Before we spoke, were you out there looking at other solutions?… How do you mean it didn't work out?" This takes them back into the pain of that history.

### Stage 5: Consequence questions (urgency, said by them)
Once they are on that emotional high, take it away. Ask about three-quarters of the way in, never earlier, because a challenging tone needs trust. "What happens if you don't do anything about this… and it keeps getting worse?" "What are the ramifications if your situation stays the same, though?" Then shift to a concern tone: "…and what would happen to [you/your job/your family] at that point?" If they say "I don't know, I'll have to figure something out," ask: "Are you… willing to settle for that?" (concerned).

### Stage 6: Qualifying questions (why now, and identity)
Make them defend urgency and commitment: "You certainly sound motivated… but for you, why look at doing this now? Why not push it down the road like a lot of [people in your position] do?" Also: "What's prompting you to look at changing this now rather than later?" "Why is that important to you now, though?" This sets up an **identity frame**. Nobody wants to identify with the people who stay stuck. Probe any non-committal answer ("I guess", "probably", "maybe") before moving on.

### Stage 7: Transition and commitment questions (bridge to close, hand to Daniel G)
Summarise their words, not yours, then ask: "Do you feel like this could be the answer for you?" If yes, ask "Hold on, why do you feel like it is, though?" (skeptical). Then "What specific aspects of what we've covered do you feel are really going to help you the most?" Then: "Well, I don't really have anything else to go over with you. It looks like we covered the bases of what you were looking for in X, Y and Z. Really the next step would be… Would that be appropriate?" Listen to the *tone* of their yes. A flat "yeah, I could see it" means go back and probe. Don't close.

## 5. Tone & delivery

Miner's five tones, used deliberately by stage:

| Tone | When | Cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Curious** | Connecting and situation questions | Light, genuinely interested ("walk me through…") |
| **Confused** | Clarifying ("Hold on, how do you mean?"), repeating emotion words | Head tilt, furrowed brow. Their brain thinks "he didn't get it, I need to explain better," so they open up |
| **Concerned** | Impact, "What's that doing to you?", consequences to family/job | Lower and softer voice, slower, lean in, hand on chest (even on the phone) |
| **Challenging / skeptical** | Consequence and qualifying questions, about ¾ of the way in only. Also the "fairly decent…" reframes | Slight doubt ("I mean… what's caused you to feel…") |
| **Playful** | Opener and small talk | Gets a laugh. Dopamine calms the nervous system |

- **Verbal pacing and pauses.** Fast questions get knee-jerk surface answers. Slow down and pause mid-question ("has that… had an impact… on you?") so they internalise it. Miner's models for this are Obama and Tony Robbins.
- **Your facial expression is "the remote control" for your tone.** You can't sound confused with a still face. Use it on Zoom and the phone alike.
- **Energy is in the middle.** Avoid the three triggers: over-excited (used-car salesman), timid/nervous (not an expert), or monotone (telemarketer reading a script).
- **Neutral words early, assumptive words later.** Early on use "maybe", "might", "possible", "could". Only become assumptive once the gap is built.

## 6. Mistakes Miner calls out (and the fix)

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Predictable openers ("How's it going?") | Pattern interrupt, a playful answer, and straight into results-based thinking |
| Assumptive frame ("if we're a good fit we'll get you started") | Neutral, downplayed frame: "pretty basic… more for us… maybe… possible next steps" |
| Generic problem questions ("biggest challenge?", "what keeps you up at night?", "what's your biggest need?") | Reframe: "I mean, you've got X… fairly decent… what's caused you to feel it might not be enough?" |
| Accepting surface answers and moving to the next scripted question | Probe loop: expand, clarify, duration, impact, deeper, root cause |
| Selling to the first problem mentioned | Keep problem-finding until there are 2–5 problems and a big gap |
| Telling the prospect their problem or why they should change | Turn it into a question so *they* say it |
| Asking feeling or challenging questions in the first minutes | Earn them. Feeling and challenge questions only after the gap and trust exist |
| Asking too fast, monotone, scripted | Verbal pacing, pauses, the right tone per question, facial expression |
| Being needy or up-playing the value | Detach. Downplay (mismatching) and push away so they pull in |
| Letting non-committal language slide ("I guess", "probably") | Listen for meaning and probe before moving on |
| Rep talks more than ~10–30% | Ask more and talk less. The prospect should talk ~90% |
| Blaming the lead ("they were broke / tyre-kickers") | Reverse-engineer it: what did I say, or how did I say it, that triggered that? |
| Relying on a one-liner close | "People don't buy because of a one-liner closing technique… they buy because of the discovery process" |

## 7. How to use this skill

**Prep mode** (given lead info or notes):
1. List the likely 3–5 problems, the personal consequences of each, and the desired objective state.
2. Write the opener frame with the lead's booked result in it.
3. Draft 2 connecting, 3 situation, 4–5 reframed problem-awareness questions (tailored to what they currently use), 2 solution-awareness, 2 consequence, and 1 qualifying question. Mark the tone for each.
4. Flag predictable traps for this lead (e.g. "will say they're the decision maker", "has a current provider").
5. Name the gap hypothesis in one line: "Current: ___ → Objective: ___ → Gap = ___."

**Live / next line mode** (given a transcript snippet):
- Identify the current NEPQ stage and what the prospect *meant*: surface, emotional, non-committal, or a buying signal.
- Give the **exact next line** (one question, max two), with tone and pacing cues in brackets. Say why it works in one sentence.
- Default rules: emotion word → repeat it back. Vague answer → "In what way, though?" or ask for a specific example. First problem named → probe it, then find the next one. Pushing for price early → don't present yet. Downplay and return to the gap (or hand to Voss if it's a true objection).

**Call review mode** (given a full transcript):
- Map the call to the stages with timestamps. Note which were skipped or rushed.
- Count the problems uncovered, how deep each was probed (surface, impact, personal), and whether the prospect or the rep said the consequences.
- Score with the scorecard below and output in the format given.
- For every end-of-call objection, name the discovery gap that caused it.

## 8. Scorecard (discovery stage)

Score each criterion 1–5.

| # | Criterion | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Opener & frame** | "How's it going?", assumptive "if we're a fit we'll get you started", over-excited or monotone | Neutral-ish frame but stock phrasing, no gap language | Pattern interrupt, downplayed neutral frame with gap language ("maybe… possible next steps… would that help you?"), results-based |
| 2 | **Situation clarity** | Skips to pitch, doesn't know their current setup | Gets basic facts | Clear picture of current approach, duration and results, in their words |
| 3 | **Problem depth (the gap)** | 0–1 problem, accepted at face value, rep sells to it | 2 problems, some probing | 3+ problems, each probed through example, duration, impact and personal impact. Prospect relives the pain |
| 4 | **Probing & clarifying** | Never follows up, jumps to next scripted question | Some "tell me more" | Consistent probe loop: repeats emotion words, "in what way though?", "what's that doing to you?", varied lead-ins |
| 5 | **Solution awareness** | No picture of the future | Asks "what do you want?" | Prospect describes the objective state and what it means for them *personally* |
| 6 | **Consequence & urgency** | Rep tells them the cost of waiting, or nothing | Asks one consequence question, no follow-up | Prospect states the consequences and why now (qualifying), asked in challenge-then-concern tone at the right time |
| 7 | **Self-persuasion ratio** | Rep talks most, makes claims, pitches mid-discovery | ~50/50, mixed | Prospect talks 80–90%. Their reasons to buy are in their own words |
| 8 | **Tonality & pacing** | Fast, flat, needy or pushy | Some pacing, tone mismatched to stage | Tones match the stage, clear verbal pauses, detached and neutral throughout |
