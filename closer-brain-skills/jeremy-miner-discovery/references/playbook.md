# NEPQ Discovery Playbook (Jeremy Miner): extended detail

Grouped by situation. Quotes are short and attributed to Miner. Everything else is a distilled paraphrase of the sources listed in `sources.md`.

---

## A. Before the call: write the "sales structure"

Miner's consulting process when he wrote scripts for companies (Scott Clary interview, YAP interview):
1. What are the prospect's **top 5 problems** that your offer solves?
2. What are the **consequences** to the prospect if each isn't solved? Go all the way to the personal level. Marketing-agency example: lack of leads → business struggles → "can't pay for their daughter's education… Grandma's nursing home."
3. How does your **solution solve** each one?
"Once I understood those three things, it's easy to write the questions."

Treat the result as a framework, like an actor's script. It's 100% scripted, but delivered with tone, face and body language so it never *sounds* scripted. Know the psychology of each stage so you can "tweak the next question based off their answer in a nanosecond." If you can't adapt, the prospect feels misunderstood and stays surface level.

**Gap hypothesis template**
- Current state (facts + feelings): ____
- Objective state (results + identity): ____
- Problems you expect (aim for 3–5): ____
- Consequences (business → personal): ____
- Likely "why now": ____

## B. The opening seconds: disarm the nervous system

**Why:** In the first 7–12 seconds the prospect reads verbal and non-verbal cues. If you come across aggressive, needy or attached, they go into fight-or-flight ("I'm too busy", "we're good", "just tell me the price"). If you come across neutral, unbiased, detached and curious, they get curious and engage. (Short: "How to Trigger Any Prospect in 12 Seconds.")

**Predictable questions to drop:** "How's it going today?", "How's the weather over there?", "Where do you live?", "Are you the homeowner?" They are pattern-matched to every salesperson and lower your status. You can ask these later, once trust exists.

**Playful replies when they ask how you are** (aim for a laugh, which releases dopamine and calms them):
- "Oh, just hanging out, being the boring guy. What about you?"
- "Just trying to stay out of trouble. You been getting in trouble over there?"
Delivery matters. Said flat, these sound weird.

**Results-based opener on an inbound Zoom:** "It looks like you booked on the calendar about, I guess, maybe [losing some weight / getting more clients]… right?" This gets them into results thinking, not price thinking.

**The neutral frame** (YAP interview, life-insurance short, Cole Gordon). Example structure:
> "Yeah, the first part of this call is pretty basic. It's really more for us to find out what you're doing now for [X] and kind of maybe the results you're getting from that, compared to maybe where you're wanting them to be, to see what that gap looks like. Then towards the end of the call, if you feel like it might be what you're looking for, we can talk about possible next steps. Would that help you?"

Why each piece is there:
- **"pretty basic"**: downplay it. Mismatching: when you downplay, they up-play, and their guard comes down.
- **"for us"**: collaborative, not "for me."
- **"the results you're getting from that"** (hand low) **"compared to maybe where you're wanting them to be"** (hand high): a visual gap.
- **"maybe / might / possible"**: neutral. Being assumptive in minute one triggers A-types ("I didn't say I'm ready to buy").
- **"Would that help you?"**: nobody says no to that.
- Don't repeat "maybe" a hundred times. Rotate "could", "possibly", "might".

**Contrast, the frame Miner says triggers resistance:** "The way this call's going to go is I'll ask you a few questions, you can ask me a few, and at the end if you feel we're a good fit and I feel you're a good fit, we'll show you how to get started. Fair enough?" Some prospects agree out loud and then stay guarded the whole call.

**Push away to pull in:**
- "After the conversation we might find you're better off just staying with who you already have." Say it at the start, never at the end.
- "Are you going to be angry at me if at the end we find we can't even help you?" ("No, I'd never be angry.") This makes them come to your aid.
- "Why should I go with you?" → "Well, I'm not quite sure you should yet." [pause 2–3 seconds] "…I might need to know a bit more about what you're using now, just to see if we could even help you in the first place." Then ask a situation question.
- Solar door demo: "Oh yeah, I can appreciate that. To be frank, it hasn't been worth being interested in… it may not even be for you, Travis… now we're getting a bit ahead of ourselves, because I don't even know if that would work for you. I'd have to know more about your usage. How long have you been in the home?"

## C. Connecting and situation questions

**Purpose of connecting questions:** take the focus off you and put it on them, disarm, and move them into results-based thinking over price- or cost-based thinking.

**Situation questions** find the *real* situation. Prospects don't know their real situation yet, and you can't build a gap from an unknown starting point. They are fact-finding, asked in a **curious** tone:
- "Can I… can I ask you something?" ("Sure, what's up?") Compare the average rep's "Let me ask you something—" which barrels them over.
- "Walk me through what you guys are doing now to [generate leads / handle X], just so I have more context."
- "How long have you been doing that?"
- Amazon-training example: "What do you do now for a living? How long have you been doing it?" Then the first problem-awareness question: "So you've been a firefighter the last 12 years… do you… like what you're doing for a living?"

**Decision process (instead of "Are you the decision maker?" / "Who else is involved?"):**
- "Can you walk me through your company's decision-making process when it comes to solving challenges like this?"
- "Can you walk me through the criteria used to make a decision on something like this?"
- "Can you go over with me what you might be looking for when choosing a company to work with, just so I understand?" This also helps position your offer later.
Never take "I'm the only decision maker" at face value in B2B (Miner cites ~6.7 influencers per enterprise decision). In B2C, gently surface the spouse or partner.

## D. Problem-awareness: the reframing formulas

**Why generic questions fail:** "The biggest reason why your prospects give you vague, generalized, surface-level answers is because you ask vague, generalized, surface-level questions." Lay-downs (~5%) answer anything. The other 95% recognise the pattern and know where it leads.

**Banned:** "What's your biggest challenge?" / "What are two problems that keep you up at night?" / "What's your biggest need right now?"

**Formula 1, "fairly decent" (skeptical/confused tone, verbal pauses):**
> "I mean… you've been with [current provider/approach] the last [time]… I mean, they're fairly decent… I mean, what's caused you to feel like you might want to look at something else?"
- Don't praise them ("great company") or trash them (you're biased). "Fairly decent" in a slightly doubtful tone seeds doubt without you saying it, and it builds trust.
- Variants from the sources:
  - Life insurance: "I mean, you've already got this $80K work policy… I mean, what's caused you to feel like that might not be enough?" This pushes them away from their current policy so they defend why it isn't enough, and it pre-empts the "I already have coverage" objection.
  - Financial advisor: "You've been with your advisor at [firm] the last 5 years… they're a fairly decent firm… what's caused you to feel their plan might not be able to retire you by 62?"
  - Marketing: "What's caused you to feel your internal marketing team might not be able to scale the business?"
  - Cars: "You already have the Toyota… that's a fairly decent car… what's caused you to feel like you might want something newer?"
  - Real estate: "Your home is… gosh, it's really beautiful… what's caused you to feel like you might want to sell it?"

**Formula 2, the "100%" question:** "So are you… 100% satisfied with the results you've been getting?" Nobody is 100% satisfied with anything. They say "Well, not 100%." You mirror "Not 100%?… What don't you like?" and they start listing problems to themselves.

**Formula 3, "doom and gloom":** "I mean, it can't be all doom and gloom over there. What do you like about what they've done for you?" Let them list the positives, then right away: "What would you change if you could, though?" That's your opening. Clarify and probe whatever comes out (e.g. "Hold on, you lost $785K? What actually happened?").

**Formula 4, the reason behind the move:**
- "What's prompting you to look at changing this now, though?"
- "Can I ask what originally led you to this decision in the first place?"

## E. The probing loop: getting below the surface

Most reps get one answer, say "Okay," and go to the next line on the script. Miner's point is that the sale is made in the follow-up.

**1. Expanded probing lead-in phrases** (rotate them, because saying "can you…" 17 times sounds like a technique):
- "Tell me more about that." / "Hold on, when you said ___, can you tell me more?"
- "Can you walk me through what you mean by that?"
- "Can you give me a specific example, so I understand that better?" This takes them back to when it happened, so they relive it.
- "Can you share with me…" / "Can you describe for me what you meant by that?"
- "Can you unwrap that for me, so I have a better understanding?" / "Unpack that for me a bit more."
- "Whoa, whoa, you're losing me. You're on page 15 and I'm still on page 3. Can you go back a few pages for me?"
- "Can you explain that to me a bit more, so I know how it affects you when that happens?"

**2. Clarifying:**
- "Hold on, what do you mean by that exactly?"
- **Emotion-word repeat**: when they say stressed, frustrated, worried, anxious, tension, pressure or concerned, say just the word back with a confused-concerned face. "Stressed?" Their subconscious reads that you didn't understand, so they explain, and that is when they open up. Practise it on your spouse or kids first.
- "When you say stress… how do you mean?"

**3. Duration:** "How long has that been going on for?" Hearing themselves say "six months" or "18 months" lands hard ("I've had this problem for this long").

**4. Impact (slow, concerned, lean in):** "So you've been having [problem] for the last 18 months… has that… had an impact on you?" For B2B: "…on the business?", then "…on you personally?" If you ask it fast you get "Nah, it's not that bad." If you ask it slow you get the truth.

**5. Deeper:** They say "You have no idea." You ask "In what way, though?" Then "What's that doing to you?"

**6. Root cause:** "Hold on, what's causing that issue?" / "What's causing this to happen?"

**Timing rule:** feeling questions ("How's that affecting you personally?") aren't for minutes 2–5. "You haven't built a big enough gap." Earn them through the facts and the problem first.

**Mini worked example (generic, B2B owner):**
- Rep: "Walk me through what you're doing now for new clients, just so I have context." (curious)
- P: "Mostly referrals, some ads."
- Rep: "Okay… and are you 100% happy with what that's producing?"
- P: "Not 100%."
- Rep: "Not 100%?… What don't you like?"
- P: "It's inconsistent. Some months are dead. It's stressful."
- Rep: "Stressful?" (confused-concerned)
- P: "Yeah, like last March we had two weeks with zero calls and I had to…"
- Rep: "How long has it been like that?"
- P: "Honestly, two years."
- Rep: "So it's been up and down for two years… has that… had an impact on you?"
- P: "Yeah, I don't sleep great, my wife asks every month if we're okay…"
- Rep: "What's that doing to you?" (soft)
…then find the second and third problems before going into solution awareness.

## F. Build a BIG gap: multiple problems

- Don't sell to the first problem. Precious-metals example: the prospect says "inflation." The rep pitches. The prospect sees on the news that inflation is falling, and the deal is dead. With five problems found, one changing headline doesn't matter.
- "Most of your prospects, when you first talk to them, don't even know they have a problem. Or they don't know how bad it really is, or the consequences." Find 2, 3 or 4 other problems they didn't realise they had, and they start to see you as the expert.
- A small gap means your price "looks like another bill." A big gap means the price looks small next to the problem.
- Two kinds of gap (Cole Gordon conversation): **pain** (below par and trying to get back) and **unfulfilled desire** (fine, but knows there's another level). Both count. With top performers, frame it as "there's always another level," not "you're bad."

## G. Solution awareness (future pacing)

- Pain alone isn't enough: "You can't just help the prospect find all these problems where they're wallowing in pain and not help them see what the future looks like once they're solved."
- Ask them to describe the objective state. "If you could change anything about this, what would it be?" "What would that look like?"
- Make it personal: "You being able to get that mortgage off your shoulders, where it doesn't destroy your credit… what would that do for you… personally?" (lower, concerned). Answer: "It'd reduce so much stress." Then repeat back "Stress?" and probe again.
- Avoid the cheesy "How would that make you feel?" unless you've built up to it.
- Past attempts: "Before we met, were you out there looking at XYZ?" "Yeah, we went with an agency, it never worked out." "Hold on, how do you mean it didn't work out?" This takes them back into the pain of that history.
- Get them on an emotional high here, because the next stage takes it away.

## H. Consequence questions (urgency they generate themselves)

- Purpose: "build urgency in that prospect's mind that they need to change their situation now, not keep pushing it down the road… We're not telling them that… our questions allow them to tell themselves."
- Generic set: "What happens if nothing changes?" / "What are the consequences if you don't do anything about this?" / "What are the ramifications if your situation stays the same, though?"
- Sequence the tone. **Challenging** first: "What happens if you don't do anything about this and you keep getting these low-quality leads and sales keep going down…" Then **concerned**: "…what would happen to your job at that point?"
- Life-insurance consequence (concerned tone): "Let's say you pass away years before you thought you would… how many months would [spouse] be able to pay the mortgage, the cars and the expenses without your income?" Said accusingly, the prospect gets defensive.
- "I don't know, I'd have to figure something out." → "Are you… willing to settle for that?" (concerned)
- Timing: about three-quarters of the way in. A challenge without trust gets defensiveness.
- Coaching note from Miner on not wanting to "make them feel pain": if they don't feel pain, they won't change and you can't help them. Concern for the consequence is the ethical stance: "I'm concerned about the consequences for them if they don't change."

## I. Qualifying: why now, plus identity

- "Well, John, you certainly sound motivated… but for you, why look at doing this now? Why not push it down the road like a lot of [companies in your industry] do, that end up [going out of business / staying stuck]?" This is an **identity frame**. Nobody wants to identify with the negative group, so they defend why they're different.
- "But why is it important for you to look at changing this now rather than pushing it down the road?"
- "[Goal] sounds good… why is that important now?"
- Cole Gordon's compare-and-contrast version: "This is who you say you want to be, but your actions are over here… when do you think is a good time to align the two?"
- Mirror their behaviour back as slightly abnormal and good, and ask them to justify it. ("Most people in your spot don't even look into this. What made you different?")

## J. Listening for meaning and non-committal language

- Active listening means "listening to what the prospect *means*, not just what they say."
- "Do you feel like this could be the answer for you?" If they say "Oh, definitely, for sure," they're in. If they say "Yeah… yeah, I could see how it could work," that's uncertainty. Don't close. Go back and find what's missing.
- Non-committal language throughout ("I guess", "probably", "it's fine", "maybe") left unprobed means "you're toast" at the end.
- Ending your statements with a question gets buy-in. "They'd do it." "Why would they do that, though?"

---

## K. Commitment questions (bridge to the close)

Miner's three commitment questions, in order:
1. "Do you feel like… this could be the answer for you?" (pause before "this")
2. On yes: "Hold on, why do you feel like it is, though?" (skeptical probe). Now they sell themselves.
3. "What specific aspects of what we've covered do you feel like are really going to help you the most?"
Optional: "Do you feel like this is something you can do to get you where you're wanting to go?"

Then the next step, said smoothly: "Well, [name], I don't really have anything else to go over with you. It looks like we covered the bases of what you were looking for in X, Y and Z. Really the next step would be [arrangement]… Would that be appropriate?" Then hand off to the closing skill.

Miner on closing lines: "People don't buy because of a one-liner closing technique at the end. They buy because of the discovery process where you built a big enough gap… they felt so much internal tension that they had to change."

## L. Tonality drills

1. **Five-tone ladder.** Take one question ("How long has that been going on?") and say it curious, confused, concerned, challenging and playful. Record it and listen back. Face first: "your facial expression is the remote control to your tonality."
2. **Pause pacing.** Mark pauses with "…" in your question list. "So this has been going on for six months… has that… had an impact on you?" Hold each pause for about 1–2 seconds.
3. **Emotion-word reflex.** For a week, at home and on calls, repeat back every emotion word you hear.
4. **Neutral-word swap.** Rewrite your opener to remove every assumptive word ("when you sign up", "you'll love") and replace it with maybe/might/possible/could.
5. **Energy calibration.** Read your opener in the three bad modes (hyped, timid, monotone) and then in the middle mode, so you can hear the difference.
6. **Hand on chest** for concerned questions, even on the phone, because body language changes tone.

## M. Demonstrations from the content (pattern references)

- **"Sell me this Rolex"** (Shorts, 1.1M and 323K views). Miner never pitches the watch. "I'm not quite sure you'd even need it" (push away). "What type of watch do you wear now?" (situation). "Sounds like you've got a lot of really nice watches… what would cause you to feel like you might want another?" (make them defend). "What level?" (probe). "Out of all the watches, what would cause you to look at this one a bit more?" Answer: "It's been on my dream board my whole life." Debrief: "I get you to defend yourself on why you want the watch… now I'm not selling you, you're selling yourself."
- Watch variant: he ties the choice to *identity*. "How do you want your clients to view you, old money or new money?… What do you think we should do?"
- **Solar door knock**: validate the objection, downplay ("may not even be for you"), "we're getting ahead of ourselves," then a situation question. "In the first 5 seconds… we have zero trust or credibility. So we have to get him to let his guard down… then build a gap."
- **Life insurance phone opener**: the neutral frame ("pretty basic… what you have in place now compared to what you might feel you need… see what that gap looks like… possible next steps… would that help you?"). "You can't just go in for the kill."
- **Commoditisation trap** (Short "Sales is about change"): when asked "Why should I go with you?", every rep answers with features and sounds the same. Only questions differentiate you.
- **Mismatching** (Short): downplay savings ("not that big of a deal, only ~$60K a year") and the A-type pulls you in ("$60K is a lot of money!").

## N. Fast diagnostic: symptom → discovery cause → fix

| End-of-call symptom | Likely discovery cause | Fix next call |
|---|---|---|
| "I need to think about it" | Gap too small, no prospect-voiced consequence | More problems. Consequence and qualifying questions |
| "I need to talk to my spouse/partner/board" | Decision process not walked through. No personal impact found | "Walk me through how decisions like this get made…" early on |
| "Just tell me the price" (early) | Opener triggered fight-or-flight (needy/excited/predictable) | Neutral frame, push away, curious tone |
| "Send me something / I'll get back to you" | Surface-level answers accepted | Probe loop on every problem |
| "It's too expensive" | Price compared to a small gap. Rep sold to one problem | Find 3–5 problems. Personal consequences |
| "We're fine with our current provider" | Generic "challenges?" question | "Fairly decent…" / "100% satisfied?" / "doom and gloom" reframes |
| Flat "yeah I could see it working" at commitment | Rep didn't listen for meaning | "Hold on, why do you feel like it would, though?" and re-probe |
